Who I am

About

I am an Assistant Professor in the Department of Computer Science and Cybersecurity at the University of Central Missouri. My research asks how networked, mobile, and cyber-physical systems can sense, decide, and defend themselves using machine learning that is fast enough for the edge and robust enough to trust. I come to security and applied AI from an electrical and computer engineering foundation in wireless and vehicular networks.

I trained in electrical engineering at Bilkent University and earned my Ph.D. at Rutgers University, where my dissertation treated the connected car as a networked sensor platform. Driver-identification research with General Motors led to U.S. Patent No. 10,332,020. Before joining UCM in 2023, I was an Assistant Professor at Bahçeşehir University and Yeditepe University in Istanbul.

Across these roles the work has kept one habit: take a signal that a system treats as noise, ask what it gives away, and build a model that can use it on modest hardware. That habit carried from the dissertation's GPS-interference work, through acoustic side channels on phones, to the intrusion-detection and IoT-identity work my group does now.

What my group works on

Research

One question runs through my work: can behavioral signatures in raw sensor streams, paired with learned models, serve as trust anchors for safety-critical networked systems?

A car's start-up routine can identify its driver. A phone's two microphones can hear which key was pressed. A single packet header can say which device sent it. Each of these results treats a signal the system ignores as evidence, then asks whether a model small and fast enough for a vehicle, a phone or a gateway can act on it. That is the thread from my doctoral work on connected cars at Rutgers to the security and applied-AI work my group does at UCM.

Three directions

01

Trustworthy edge AI for connected vehicles

Cars take in data from roadside units, other vehicles and the cloud, and each channel can be spoofed. Building on the GPS-interference detection from my dissertation and the in-vehicle signal analysis behind the GM patent, this direction catches spoofed, jammed or manipulated sensor and V2X data on hardware where latency and memory are tight.

  • Sensing, identity and trust · extended vision · driver scoring
  • GNSS spoofing · in-vehicle network · V2X ghosts · location privacy · EV charging
02

Lightweight and quantum-enhanced intrusion detection

Our hybrid quantum-classical network detects DDoS traffic and classifies Android malware in one framework. The question that matters is not whether a quantum circuit beats a classical one today, but whether hybrid and compressed models stay accurate inside the budget of an IoT gateway, and whether we can explain what they learned.

  • Hybrid quantum-classical detection · quantum spectroscopy
  • Honest quantum benchmarks · TinyML · checkable explanations · federated detection · realistic evasion
03

Device identity and integrity in IoT

A network that cannot say which physical device sent a packet, or whether that device was tampered with, cannot defend itself. We identify devices from single packet headers and name network incidents with genetic-algorithm feature selection. Next is pairing traffic fingerprints with ledger-based attestation, cheaply enough for constrained devices.

  • Device identity and incidents
  • Ledger identity · leak-free benchmarks · encrypted traffic · radio fingerprints

Recently submitted

Student-led papers from my group that are submitted and not yet published.

Enhancing university chatbots with hybrid multi-query retrieval-augmented generation: a case study at UCM

L. N. Posini, R. Siddiqua, G. Kar · led by UCM graduate students Lokesh Narayana Posini and Raqshanda Siddiqua

Students lose time hunting for answers spread across a university website. We built a retrieval-augmented chatbot for UCM from the university's own pages and FAQs and compared two ways of finding the passages it answers from: vector search alone, and a hybrid that adds BM25 keyword search and has a language model (Gemini) rewrite each question into several queries. The hybrid found more of the relevant passages, at a small cost in how neatly they were ranked.

Submitted

One knob, two objectives: group parity, benefit inequality, and the reliability of fairness tuning

T. Nepal, G. Kar · led by UCM graduate student Tanushree Nepal

Machine learning pipelines often treat fairness as a setting to tune. We use one training loss whose weight slides the fairness penalty between two goals, equal outcomes across demographic groups and an even spread of benefit across individuals, and train thousands of models on six tabular datasets to measure how strongly the two goals pull against each other. The paper also shows when tuning fairness on a small validation set can fail without warning.

Submitted

The evolution of object detection: a systematic review of transformer-based and few-shot learning approaches

M. S. I. Khan, G. Kar · led by UCM student Md Shakiful Islam Khan

Object detection has moved from convolutional detectors to transformers that treat finding objects as a set-prediction problem, and to few-shot methods that learn new object types from a handful of labeled examples. This systematic review traces that shift, compares the main transformer-based and few-shot families and the benchmarks they are judged on, and sets out the open challenges, including the move toward foundation and vision-language models.

Students and mentoring

I have supervised six completed M.Sc. theses; four became journal articles with the student as lead author, in Applied Soft Computing, Sensors, Journal of Engineering Sciences and Design and Expert Systems.

Each thesis starts as a small production pipeline: a publishable research question, a dataset, a reproducible experiment and a target venue, agreed at the proposal stage.

Joining the group. I welcome UCM graduate students interested in security, networked systems and applied machine learning. Pick one of the open projects, read two papers from its list, and email me with your CV and the research question you would start with.

Writing papers. Every project page has a "Start here" plan with tools and datasets. When it is time to write, the LaTeX and Overleaf guide covers templates, citations, figures and the checks before you submit.

Courses and course sites

Teaching

Students learn computing by building it on real problems.

Students learn computing by building with real data. Every graduate course I teach comes with interactive, browser-based notes, so students can run SQL, Python, and algorithms live as they read, with nothing to install. In machine learning that means training and debugging on a messy, real dataset; in algorithms, proving a time bound and then watching it in running code; in databases, designing a schema that survives real queries. My research comes into the classroom too: intrusion detection, IoT fingerprinting and connected vehicles are case studies, and Advanced Algorithms opens by turning GPS navigation into a graph and rerunning Dijkstra's algorithm as traffic changes.
