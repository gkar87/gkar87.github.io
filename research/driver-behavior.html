Scoring and coaching drivers from in-vehicle data

The same in-vehicle data that identifies a driver can also score how they drive, as they drive, and turn that score into feedback people act on.

The question

Can a car's own data score how someone drives during the trip, and can that score change how they drive?

Why it matters

Insurers and fleets often score drivers from coarse data after the trip, when feedback comes too late to matter.

What we found

Scotto scores driving in real time from in-vehicle data, and in the gamification study, results on real-world and simulated trips agreed more than 95% of the time.

Scotto: scoring driving in real time

Insurers and fleet operators score drivers, but often from coarse data, after the trip. Scotto (IEEE VTC2019-Spring), with B. Asiroglu and F. S. Bir, scores driver behavior in real time from the car's own in-vehicle data, so feedback can arrive while it still matters. The live figure above shows the basic idea with made-up thresholds: flag harsh braking and harsh starts from acceleration, and turn them into a score.

Gamification: making the score matter

A score alone rarely changes how someone drives. In Improving driver behavior using gamification (MobiWIS 2018), with S. Helvaci, A. Senova and S. Gören, we read in-vehicle data, scored driving errors on several criteria, and wrapped the score in achievements and leaderboards, with a feature that points out nearby places of interest. We tested it on two real-world trips and compared them with simulated ones.

Discussion

What it shows

In-vehicle data can score driving as it happens, and simulated trips agreed closely with real ones. Wrapping the score in achievements gives drivers a reason to act on it.

Where it falls short

  • The gamification study tested two real-world trips, so lasting behavior change needs longer field studies.
  • What counts as safe driving depends on the road and the traffic, so thresholds need care.
  • Driving data is personal, which makes it matter where the scoring runs.

What it means

Insurers and fleets already want driver scores. Computing them on the vehicle keeps the raw data in the car.

Start here

A first month for a student joining this work, in 3 steps. Do them in order: each one uses what the one before produced.

Goal for the month: A working harsh-event detector on real driving data, compared against a small learned model.

Before you start: setup for every project
  1. Set up a workspace. Create a private Git repository for the project with folders for data/, src/, figures/ and notes/. Make a Python virtual environment (python -m venv .venv) and record every package you install in requirements.txt.
  2. Keep a lab notebook. Add a dated entry to notes/log.md each time you work: what you ran (the exact command), what came out, and what surprised you. This log is the first thing to share when you ask for help, and most of your paper's method section comes from it.
  3. Make every number reproducible. Fix random seeds, write down the dataset version and how you split it, and keep the script that produced each figure next to the figure. If you cannot regenerate a number with one command, it is not finished.
  4. Stay legal and ethical. Work only with public datasets, data you recorded yourself with everyone's consent, or systems you own. Never test an attack on a network, car, radio band or service that is not yours, and follow the safety note on this page.
  5. When you get stuck. Spend about an hour, then write down what you tried, what you expected and the exact error or plot, and ask. A precise question is much easier to answer than "it does not work".
  1. Step 1 · Week 1List what gets scored
    • Read the papers below.
    • Make a table of the events each scoring rule counts (hard braking, sharp turns, speeding) and the thresholds it uses, where the paper gives them.

    You are done when your table covers every paper and notes where they disagree.

  2. Step 2 · Weeks 2 and 3Detect events with a threshold
    • Load several comma2k19 drives and extract speed, and compute acceleration from it.
    • Flag harsh braking and acceleration with a simple threshold, and plot each drive with its events marked.
    • Check a handful of flagged events by eye: are they real, or noise from the signal?

    You are done when you have a plot per drive and a short note on false events you found.

  3. Step 3 · Week 4Compare with a classifier
    • Label a small set of events by hand and train a small scikit-learn classifier on windows of speed and acceleration.
    • Score every drive with both methods and report how much the score changes from drive to drive under each.

    You are done when one table compares the two methods, with the labeled set saved so the result can be reproduced.

Tools and data
