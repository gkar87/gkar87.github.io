Extending what a car can see and sense

A car's own sensors stop at the truck in front of it, and the phone in the cabin knows more about the road than it seems. This work shares views between vehicles and turns phone and car sensors into a picture of the road.

The question

Can a car see past the truck in front of it, and can the phone in the cabin read the road?

Why it matters

Sensors stop at line of sight, so a stopped car or a pedestrian hidden behind a truck stays invisible until it is too late.

What we found

Nearby cars sharing their sensor views let a car see what was hidden, and phone and car sensors recognized lane changes with over 90% accuracy, more reliably when many drives were combined.

Seeing past line of sight: augmented vehicular reality

A camera or lidar sees only what is in its line of sight, so a stopped car or a pedestrian hidden behind a truck stays invisible until it is too late. In Augmented vehicular reality (HotMobile 2017), with Hang Qiu, Fawad Ahmad, Ramesh Govindan, Marco Gruteser and Fan Bai, nearby vehicles share what their sensors see, so a car can effectively look through the vehicle in front of it.

  1. Senseeach car builds a view of its surroundings
  2. Sharenearby cars exchange views over a wireless link
  3. Alignplace the other car's view in your own frame
  4. Extendobjects hidden from you appear in your view

Making this work means sending enough sensor data quickly over a wireless link and placing another car's view correctly in your own. The live figure above shows only the geometry: what is hidden behind the truck and what the truck's sensors can add.

Sensing the road from phones and cars

In Towards robust vehicular context sensing (IEEE Transactions on Vehicular Technology, 2018), we detected lane changes, potholes, road grade and stop signs from sensors that are already in the car and in the driver's phone, and compared what phone sensors and the car's own sensors can each deliver. Combining many drives over the same road (crowd-sensing) makes the hard cases more reliable.

>90%accuracy of lane-change determinations
+35%recall for lane changes on curves when many drives are combined

Data: Qiu et al., IEEE TVT 2018, abstract.

Discussion

What it shows

Sharing sensor views lets a car see what is hidden behind the vehicle in front, and phone and car sensors together recognized lane changes, potholes, road grade and stop signs, more reliably when many drives were combined.

Where it falls short

  • Shared views only help if they arrive quickly and are placed correctly in the receiving car's frame.
  • Every shared view is also a new input that an attacker could fake.
  • Where the phone sits in the cabin changes what its sensors can see.

What it means

Cooperative perception needs a trust check before a car acts on another car's view. Catching ghost vehicles in V2X messages is that check.

Start here

A first month for a student joining this work, in 3 steps. Do them in order: each one uses what the one before produced.

Goal for the month: A simulated occlusion scene and a first measurement of how much a shared view helps and what it costs.

Before you start: setup for every project
  1. Set up a workspace. Create a private Git repository for the project with folders for data/, src/, figures/ and notes/. Make a Python virtual environment (python -m venv .venv) and record every package you install in requirements.txt.
  2. Keep a lab notebook. Add a dated entry to notes/log.md each time you work: what you ran (the exact command), what came out, and what surprised you. This log is the first thing to share when you ask for help, and most of your paper's method section comes from it.
  3. Make every number reproducible. Fix random seeds, write down the dataset version and how you split it, and keep the script that produced each figure next to the figure. If you cannot regenerate a number with one command, it is not finished.
  4. Stay legal and ethical. Work only with public datasets, data you recorded yourself with everyone's consent, or systems you own. Never test an attack on a network, car, radio band or service that is not yours, and follow the safety note on this page.
  5. When you get stuck. Spend about an hour, then write down what you tried, what you expected and the exact error or plot, and ask. A precise question is much easier to answer than "it does not work".
  1. Step 1 · Week 1Understand the geometry
    • Read the papers below.
    • Draw one occlusion case by hand: a car, a truck in front of it, and a pedestrian or car hidden behind the truck. Mark what each car's sensors can and cannot see.

    You are done when your sketch shows the blind zone and the extra area a second car would reveal.

  2. Step 2 · Weeks 2 and 3Build the scene in a simulator
    • Install CARLA on a machine with a recent graphics card, and run one of its example scripts.
    • Place two cars behind a truck and attach a camera or LiDAR sensor to each.
    • Record a few seconds from both cars and save the sensor output with timestamps.

    You are done when you can replay the recording and see the hidden object only in the second car's view.

  3. Step 3 · Week 4Measure benefit and cost
    • Estimate how much of the blind zone the shared view removes, for example the share of hidden objects that become visible.
    • Estimate the data each car would have to send per second to share that view, raw and in a compressed or object-list form.

    You are done when you have one table with blind-zone coverage and bandwidth for each sharing option.
