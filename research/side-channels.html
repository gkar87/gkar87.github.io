Acoustic side channels on phones

A phone lying behind your keyboard can hear which keys you press. We showed how, with no training data and no language model, which means it works on random passwords too.

The question

Can one phone lying near a keyboard tell which keys you press, from sound alone?

Why it matters

Microphones are everywhere, and nobody thinks of them as a way to steal a password.

What we found

Yes. Timing each keystroke at the phone's two microphones placed it to within millimeters; the first guess was right for 85.5% of keystrokes at 48 kHz and 94.2% at 192 kHz on one keyboard, with no training data.

Snooping keystrokes with millimeter-level audio ranging

Every key sits at a slightly different distance from a phone's two microphones, so its sound reaches them a fraction of a millisecond apart. Measuring that time difference of arrival places the keystroke on the keyboard. Sound features then separate keys that sit at similar distances.

  1. Detectfind each keystroke by its sound energy
  2. Time differencecross-correlate the two microphones
  3. Groupkeys with similar time differences
  4. Clusterby sound features within each group
  5. Labelmatch clusters to key positions

The lesson for defenders: sensors a system does not treat as security-relevant can leak secrets. The same thinking now drives my work on vehicle and network signals.

Discussion

What it shows

An ordinary phone's two microphones located keystrokes precisely enough to recover what was typed, with no training on the victim and no language model, so random passwords leak too.

Where it falls short

  • The attack assumed a phone close to the keyboard and a clean, high-rate recording.
  • Keyboard model, typing style and background noise all change accuracy.
  • Calls and earbuds compress audio and suppress noise, which may remove the cues or keep them. That is still open.

What it means

Any microphone near a keyboard is a possible leak, and sensors a system does not treat as security-relevant deserve a threat model too.

Open research questions

Questions this work leaves open. They are directions, not results; each links to the planned project that takes it up.

  1. Do earbuds, smart speakers and video-call apps still leak keystrokes after compression and noise suppression?

    Why it is open
    The original attack used a phone recording at a high sample rate. Today's microphones sit in very different audio pipelines.

Start here

A first month for a student joining this work, in 3 steps. Do them in order: each one uses what the one before produced.

Goal for the month: Measured time differences for real key presses, checked against the geometry from our keystroke paper.

Before you start: setup for every project
  1. Set up a workspace. Create a private Git repository for the project with folders for data/, src/, figures/ and notes/. Make a Python virtual environment (python -m venv .venv) and record every package you install in requirements.txt.
  2. Keep a lab notebook. Add a dated entry to notes/log.md each time you work: what you ran (the exact command), what came out, and what surprised you. This log is the first thing to share when you ask for help, and most of your paper's method section comes from it.
  3. Make every number reproducible. Fix random seeds, write down the dataset version and how you split it, and keep the script that produced each figure next to the figure. If you cannot regenerate a number with one command, it is not finished.
  4. Stay legal and ethical. Work only with public datasets, data you recorded yourself with everyone's consent, or systems you own. Never test an attack on a network, car, radio band or service that is not yours, and follow the safety note on this page.
  5. When you get stuck. Spend about an hour, then write down what you tried, what you expected and the exact error or plot, and ask. A precise question is much easier to answer than "it does not work".
  1. Step 1 · Week 1Work the geometry
    • Read the keystroke ranging paper below (ACM MobiCom 2015).
    • For one key on a real keyboard, measure its distance to two microphone positions and compute the expected time difference at the speed of sound.

    You are done when you have the expected time difference for one key, with your measurements shown.

  2. Step 2 · Week 2Find key presses in audio
    • Record yourself pressing a few keys with a phone placed next to the keyboard.
    • Load the audio with librosa, detect each press (for example with onset detection), and plot the waveform with the presses marked.

    You are done when every press you made is marked on the plot, and no extra ones are.

  3. Step 3 · Weeks 3 and 4Measure and compare
    • Record the same keys with two microphones at once (for example a phone with two microphones or two synchronized recorders).
    • Measure the time difference for each press by cross-correlation and compare it with the geometry from week 1.

    You are done when one table lists expected vs measured time difference for each key, with the error.
