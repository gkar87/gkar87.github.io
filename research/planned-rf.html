Telling radios apart by their imperfections

No two radios are built exactly alike. Tiny flaws in each transmitter leave a signature in every signal it sends. This project asks whether those signatures can identify IoT devices reliably, even as the radio moves and conditions change.

Why this matters

A device's address and certificates can be copied; the small analog flaws of its radio are much harder to copy. Radiometric fingerprinting uses those flaws to tell transmitters apart, and deep learning has made it far more accurate. It also made it fragile: a classifier can quietly learn the wireless channel instead of the radio, and fail as soon as the device moves.

My work on device identity reads fingerprints from packet headers (ICISSP 2024), and my wireless background goes back to resource allocation in cellular networks (M.S. thesis, Bilkent University) and physical-layer security in vehicles (ACM CCS 2014). This project adds the radio layer to device identity, with low-cost software-defined radios.

Research questions

  1. Which radio imperfections give a stable fingerprint for low-cost IoT radios over days and changing conditions?

    Hypothesis
    Frequency offset and IQ imbalance give the most stable cues, but some of them shift with temperature and supply voltage.
    How we would test it
    Record the same devices over days, temperatures and battery levels, and measure how far each feature drifts compared with the gap between devices.

  2. Does a fingerprint learned in one place still work after the radio moves to another, and how can the channel be kept out of it?

    Hypothesis
    Classifiers trained in a single environment partly learn the channel; training across channels, or removing the channel first, keeps more accuracy after a move.
    How we would test it
    Train in one room or day and test in another, with and without channel equalization and augmentation, and report the drop in accuracy.

  3. Can RF fingerprints and traffic fingerprints check each other, so that a cloned device must fake both?

    Hypothesis
    Requiring both the radio signature and the packet-level behavior to match makes cloning a device much harder than either check alone.
    How we would test it
    Build both fingerprints for the same devices, then stage cloning and replay attacks that fake one of them and measure detection.

Expected contributions

  • A multi-day, multi-place RF fingerprint dataset from low-cost IoT radios.
  • Measurements of which radio features stay stable as conditions change.
  • A joint radio-and-traffic identity check, tested against cloning.

Approach

  1. RecordCapture signals from many identical IoT radios with software-defined receivers.
  2. VaryRepeat across days, rooms, distances, temperatures and battery levels.
  3. ModelCompare hand-made impairment features with deep models on raw IQ samples.
  4. SeparateTest channel equalization and augmentation to keep the channel out of the fingerprint.
  5. CombinePair the radio fingerprint with the device's traffic fingerprint and test against clones.

Receivers only listen to our own lab devices on unlicensed bands, within the rules for those bands.

Discussion

If it works

Devices are recognized by their radios, which a cloned software identity cannot copy.

Where it could fail

  • Signatures shift with temperature, movement and receiver, so models may not transfer.
  • Collecting labeled signals needs radio hardware and a careful setup.

How we will know

Identification accuracy across days, positions and receivers, not only within one recording.

Start here

A concrete first month for a student joining this work. Each step builds on the one before.

  1. Step 1Read the papers below, starting with the radiometric signatures paper.
  2. Step 2Download part of the ORACLE dataset and plot constellations for two radios.
  3. Step 3Train a classifier on one recording session and test it on another to see the channel effect.
  4. Step 4Work through the GNU Radio tutorials to plan your own recordings.

  2. ORACLE: Optimized radio classification through convolutional neural networks
    K. Sankhe, M. Belgiovine, F. Zhou, S. Riyaz, S. Ioannidis, K. Chowdhury
    IEEE INFOCOM, 2019

  3. Exposing the fingerprint: Dissecting the impact of the wireless channel on radio fingerprinting
    A. Al-Shawabka, F. Restuccia et al.
