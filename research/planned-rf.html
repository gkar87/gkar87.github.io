Telling radios apart by their imperfections

No two radios are built exactly alike. Tiny flaws in each transmitter leave a signature in every signal it sends. This project asks whether those signatures can identify IoT devices reliably, even as the radio moves and conditions change.

The question

Can tiny flaws in each radio identify IoT devices reliably, even when the device moves and conditions change?

Why it matters

Addresses and certificates can be copied; the analog flaws of a radio are much harder to fake.

How we will test it

Record the same devices over days, temperatures and battery levels, train in one place and test in another, and stage cloning attacks that fake one fingerprint.

Why this matters

A device's address and certificates can be copied; the small analog flaws of its radio are much harder to copy. Radiometric fingerprinting uses those flaws to tell transmitters apart, and deep learning has made it far more accurate. It also made it fragile: a classifier can quietly learn the wireless channel instead of the radio, and fail as soon as the device moves.

My work on device identity reads fingerprints from packet headers (ICISSP 2024), and my wireless background goes back to resource allocation in cellular networks (M.S. thesis, Bilkent University) and physical-layer security in vehicles (ACM CCS 2014). This project adds the radio layer to device identity, with low-cost software-defined radios.

Research questions

  1. Which radio imperfections give a stable fingerprint for low-cost IoT radios over days and changing conditions?

    Hypothesis
    Frequency offset and IQ imbalance give the most stable cues, but some of them shift with temperature and supply voltage.
    How we would test it
    Record the same devices over days, temperatures and battery levels, and measure how far each feature drifts compared with the gap between devices.

  2. Does a fingerprint learned in one place still work after the radio moves to another, and how can the channel be kept out of it?

    Hypothesis
    Classifiers trained in a single environment partly learn the channel; training across channels, or removing the channel first, keeps more accuracy after a move.
    How we would test it
    Train in one room or day and test in another, with and without channel equalization and augmentation, and report the drop in accuracy.

  3. Can RF fingerprints and traffic fingerprints check each other, so that a cloned device must fake both?

    Hypothesis
    Requiring both the radio signature and the packet-level behavior to match makes cloning a device much harder than either check alone.
    How we would test it
    Build both fingerprints for the same devices, then stage cloning and replay attacks that fake one of them and measure detection.

Expected contributions

  • A multi-day, multi-place RF fingerprint dataset from low-cost IoT radios.
  • Measurements of which radio features stay stable as conditions change.
  • A joint radio-and-traffic identity check, tested against cloning.

Approach

  1. RecordCapture signals from many identical IoT radios with software-defined receivers.
  2. VaryRepeat across days, rooms, distances, temperatures and battery levels.
  3. ModelCompare hand-made impairment features with deep models on raw IQ samples.
  4. SeparateTest channel equalization and augmentation to keep the channel out of the fingerprint.
  5. CombinePair the radio fingerprint with the device's traffic fingerprint and test against clones.

Receivers only listen to our own lab devices on unlicensed bands, within the rules for those bands.

Discussion

If it works

Devices are recognized by their radios, which a cloned software identity cannot copy.

Where it could fail

  • Signatures shift with temperature, movement and receiver, so models may not transfer.
  • Collecting labeled signals needs radio hardware and a careful setup.

How we will know

Identification accuracy across days, positions and receivers, not only within one recording.

Start here

A first month for a student joining this work, in 4 steps. Do them in order: each one uses what the one before produced.

Goal for the month: A radio fingerprinting classifier and a measured drop when it meets a new recording session.

Before you start: setup for every project
  1. Set up a workspace. Create a private Git repository for the project with folders for data/, src/, figures/ and notes/. Make a Python virtual environment (python -m venv .venv) and record every package you install in requirements.txt.
  2. Keep a lab notebook. Add a dated entry to notes/log.md each time you work: what you ran (the exact command), what came out, and what surprised you. This log is the first thing to share when you ask for help, and most of your paper's method section comes from it.
  3. Make every number reproducible. Fix random seeds, write down the dataset version and how you split it, and keep the script that produced each figure next to the figure. If you cannot regenerate a number with one command, it is not finished.
  4. Stay legal and ethical. Work only with public datasets, data you recorded yourself with everyone's consent, or systems you own. Never test an attack on a network, car, radio band or service that is not yours, and follow the safety note on this page.
  5. When you get stuck. Spend about an hour, then write down what you tried, what you expected and the exact error or plot, and ask. A precise question is much easier to answer than "it does not work".
  1. Step 1 · Week 1Read the papers
    • Read the papers below, starting with the radiometric signatures paper.
    • Write down which hardware flaws make two radios of the same model look different.

    You are done when you can name at least three such flaws.

  2. Step 2 · Week 2Look at the signals
    • Download part of the ORACLE dataset.
    • Plot constellations for two radios and compare them.

    You are done when your plot shows a visible difference, or a note on why there is none.

  3. Step 3 · Week 3Train and test across sessions
    • Train a classifier on one recording session and test it on the same session and on another.
    • Record both accuracies.

    You are done when one table shows same-session vs new-session accuracy.

  4. Step 4 · Week 4Plan your own recordings
    • Work through the GNU Radio tutorials.
    • Write a short plan for recording your own radios, cabled or in a shielded setup, without transmitting on licensed bands.

    You are done when you have a one-page recording plan.

Papers to read first

  1. Wireless device identification with radiometric signatures
    V. Brik, S. Banerjee, M. Gruteser, S. Oh
    ACM MobiCom, 2008
    The founding paper on radiometric fingerprints.
    Read
  2. ORACLE: Optimized radio classification through convolutional neural networks
    K. Sankhe, M. Belgiovine, F. Zhou, S. Riyaz, S. Ioannidis, K. Chowdhury
    IEEE INFOCOM, 2019
    Deep learning on raw IQ samples from identical radios.
    Read
  3. Exposing the fingerprint: Dissecting the impact of the wireless channel on radio fingerprinting
    A. Al-Shawabka, F. Restuccia et al.
    IEEE INFOCOM, 2020
    How the channel fools fingerprint classifiers, the central problem here.
    Read
  4. Comparative analysis of feature selection algorithms for automated IoT device fingerprinting
    A. Aksoy, S. Varma, G. Moorthy, E. Pan, G. Kar
    ICISSP, 2024
    Our packet-level fingerprints, the other half of the joint check.
    Read
  5. Performance of static and adaptive subchannel allocation schemes for fractional frequency reuse in WiMAX networks
    G. Kar (advisor: E. Karaşan)
    M.S. thesis, Bilkent University, 2011
    My wireless background, for the physical-layer side.
