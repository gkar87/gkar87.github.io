Connected vehicles: sensing, identity and trust

Cars are rolling sensor platforms. This line of work asks what their data can reveal, who is driving, and how to catch the signals that put everyone on the road at risk.

The question

Can data that cars and phones already produce single out a car jamming GPS, or tell who is behind the wheel?

Why it matters

One cheap jammer knocks out GPS for everyone nearby, and knowing who is driving matters for safety, insurance and theft.

What we found

Two roadside monitoring points together picked out the jamming car in every highway test pass, and the rhythm of starting the car identified the driver with over 90% accuracy within 20 seconds.

Finding the car with the GPS jammer

Cheap personal GPS jammers, plugged into a car's lighter socket, knock out GPS for everyone nearby, including aircraft and emergency systems. Locating the moving source is hard because the signal is strong only for a few seconds. We built a system that combines roadside monitoring points (a software-defined radio, a directional antenna and a camera) with ordinary smartphones, which already report GPS signal strength.

The key idea: cars drive at different speeds, so the group of cars near the jammer keeps changing. One monitoring point leaves a few suspects; a second point a few miles on leaves only the one car that was near the peak both times.

Who is driving? PredriveID and a GM patent

Before a car even moves, each driver performs the same small ritual in their own rhythm: close the door, start the engine, shift out of park, buckle up, release the brake. Working with General Motors Research, we showed that the time gaps between these events, read from the car's own data bus, identify the driver within seconds, with no camera and nothing the driver has to carry.

This work led to U.S. Patent No. 10,332,020, Method and apparatus of differentiating drivers based on driving behaviors (GM Global Technology Operations and Rutgers, 2019).

Traffic from dash cams at the vehicle edge

Dash-cam video from ordinary cars can estimate traffic in real time: we detect and count the vehicles in view and estimate their speed and lane on the vehicle itself, so the estimate arrives while it is still useful.

Distracted driving from cameras and car sensors

With my M.Sc. student Furkan Omerustaoglu, we detected nine distracted-driving behaviors with a two-stage model: convolutional networks read the driver-facing camera, and LSTM networks combine those image features with in-vehicle sensor data. Adding the sensor data significantly improved detection over images alone.

Where this is going

Connected vehicles now trust data from roadside units, other cars and the cloud, and all three can be spoofed. The next step is detecting spoofed, jammed or manipulated sensor and V2X data in real time on the vehicle and roadside hardware itself, where latency and memory are tight.

Discussion

What it shows

Signals that are already there, from GPS signal strength on phones to the rhythm of a door, an ignition and a seat belt, were enough to single out a jamming car and to recognize a driver. No new sensor had to be built into the car.

Where it falls short

  • The field tests were modest: highway passes with an emulated jammer, and a few hundred trips for driver identification. Bigger and more varied fleets would show how far the results carry.
  • A driver who knows about the system can change their routine, so pre-trip identification is a strong hint, not proof of identity.
  • Roadside cameras that photograph suspects raise privacy questions of their own.

What it means

For automakers and fleets, cheap signals that are already present can carry security value before new hardware is added. The open projects test the same idea against an attacker who is trying to fool the car.

Open research questions

Questions this work leaves open. They are directions, not results; each links to the planned project that takes it up.

  1. Can a vehicle catch spoofed, jammed or manipulated sensor and V2X data in real time, on hardware where latency and memory are tight?

    Why it is open
    Cars now trust data from roadside units, other vehicles and the cloud, and every one of those channels can be spoofed.
    Taken up in
    GNSS spoofing · ghost vehicles in V2X · in-vehicle network

  2. Can behavioral signatures in raw sensor streams, like the pre-trip rhythm that identifies a driver, serve as trust anchors for the data a car relies on?

    Why it is open
    PredriveID showed that bus signals carry a stable signature of who is driving. Whether the same kind of signature can vouch for the data itself is open.
    Taken up in
    GNSS spoofing · in-vehicle network

  3. How can cars share location data for traffic services without revealing where their drivers live and work?

    Why it is open
    My dissertation addressed data availability and privacy for connected cars. Location traces remain one of the most identifying kinds of data a car produces.
    Taken up in
    vehicle location privacy · EV charging security

Start here

A first month for a student joining this work, in 4 steps. Do them in order: each one uses what the one before produced.

Goal for the month: A one-page research plan for one open question on this page, backed by a real drive and a simulated road you have looked at yourself.

Before you start: setup for every project
  1. Set up a workspace. Create a private Git repository for the project with folders for data/, src/, figures/ and notes/. Make a Python virtual environment (python -m venv .venv) and record every package you install in requirements.txt.
  2. Keep a lab notebook. Add a dated entry to notes/log.md each time you work: what you ran (the exact command), what came out, and what surprised you. This log is the first thing to share when you ask for help, and most of your paper's method section comes from it.
  3. Make every number reproducible. Fix random seeds, write down the dataset version and how you split it, and keep the script that produced each figure next to the figure. If you cannot regenerate a number with one command, it is not finished.
  4. Stay legal and ethical. Work only with public datasets, data you recorded yourself with everyone's consent, or systems you own. Never test an attack on a network, car, radio band or service that is not yours, and follow the safety note on this page.
  5. When you get stuck. Spend about an hour, then write down what you tried, what you expected and the exact error or plot, and ask. A precise question is much easier to answer than "it does not work".
  1. Step 1 · Week 1Read with a purpose
    • Read the GPS-jammer (ACM CCS 2014) and PredriveID (ACM/IEEE SEC 2017) papers in the list below.
    • For each, write one paragraph: who the attacker or subject is, what they can and cannot do, and what data the system needs.

    You are done when you have two paragraphs that name each paper's assumptions in your own words.

  2. Step 2 · Week 2Look at real vehicle data
    • Download the example segment from the comma2k19 repository and load its logs in Python.
    • Plot GNSS speed and wheel speed for the same minute of driving on one chart, with time on the x-axis.
    • Note where the two disagree and suggest why (sampling rate, lag, tire size, GNSS dropouts).

    You are done when your chart is in figures/ with a two-sentence caption explaining the gap.

  3. Step 3 · Week 3Simulate a road
    • Install Eclipse SUMO and build a short highway in its network editor, or use one of its tutorial networks.
    • Run it with --fcd-output so every vehicle's position and speed is logged at each time step.
    • Plot two vehicles' tracks from the log. This is the raw material a roadside detector would work from.

    You are done when you can regenerate the log and the plot with one command.

  4. Step 4 · Week 4Write a one-page plan
    • Pick one open question at the bottom of this page.
    • Write one page: the question, the data you will use, the method, the baseline you will compare against, and the number that will tell you it worked.

    You are done when the plan fits on one page and someone else could start from it.
