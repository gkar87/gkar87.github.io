Training intrusion detectors without pooling the data

Every gateway sees different traffic, and most cannot share it. This project trains a shared intrusion detector across many sites while the data stays where it was collected, and asks what happens when one of those sites is malicious.

The question

Can gateways train one intrusion detector together without pooling their traffic, even when one of them is malicious?

Why it matters

Traffic is private and often cannot leave the site that recorded it, and any site can send a poisoned update.

How we will test it

Split public IoT intrusion datasets across simulated gateways, compare federated, local and pooled models, and measure how few poisoned sites it takes to plant a backdoor under each aggregation rule.

Why this matters

An intrusion detector learns best from many networks, but traffic is private and often cannot leave the site that recorded it. Federated learning trains one model across sites by sending model updates instead of data. It brings two new problems. Sites see very different traffic, so one shared model may serve some of them badly, and any site can send a poisoned update that plants a backdoor in everyone's detector.

My group's detectors are built to be small enough for IoT gateways (EPJ Quantum Technology, 2025; ICISSP 2024). Gateways are also the natural places to train federated models, so this project connects the lightweight-detection line with the device-identity line: who sends an update matters as much as what is in it.

Research questions

  1. Does a federated intrusion detector trained across many gateways match one trained on pooled data, and how does that change as sites see more different traffic?

    Hypothesis
    With mildly skewed data the federated model comes close to one trained on pooled data; as each site's traffic becomes more distinct, the gap widens unless each site keeps a small local layer.
    How we would test it
    Split public IoT intrusion datasets across simulated gateways with controlled skew, and compare federated, local-only and pooled models on held-out sites and held-out attack types.

  2. How few poisoned clients does it take to plant a backdoor in a federated detector, and which aggregation rules stop it without hurting honest sites that look unusual?

    Hypothesis
    A single client that scales its update can steer plain averaging; median-style rules resist it, but they also drift away from minority sites whose normal traffic looks unusual.
    How we would test it
    Run poisoning and backdoor attacks with a growing share of malicious clients, and measure attack success and clean accuracy for each aggregation rule, including on minority sites.

  3. Can federated training fit the bandwidth and compute of real IoT gateways?

    Hypothesis
    Compressed updates and fewer, larger local steps keep traffic small enough for gateway uplinks with little loss in detection.
    How we would test it
    Profile training rounds on gateway-class boards and measure upload size, round time and energy against detection quality.

Expected contributions

  • A federated intrusion-detection benchmark with controlled data skew across sites.
  • A measured comparison of aggregation rules under poisoning and backdoor attacks, including their cost to minority sites.
  • Gateway-level profiles of training traffic, time and energy.

Approach

  1. SplitDivide public IoT intrusion datasets across simulated sites with controlled skew.
  2. TrainRun federated averaging and robust aggregation rules in an open-source framework.
  3. AttackAdd poisoning and backdoor clients with growing share and stealth.
  4. DefendCompare robust aggregation, update checks and client reputation.
  5. DeployProfile rounds on gateway-class boards.

Attacks run only inside our own simulations and lab devices.

Discussion

If it works

Sites share what they learn without sharing their traffic.

Where it could fail

  • Sites see very different traffic, which can stall training.
  • A malicious site can poison the shared model.

How we will know

Accuracy against a model trained on pooled data, robustness with poisoned sites, and communication cost.

Start here

A first month for a student joining this work, in 3 steps. Do them in order: each one uses what the one before produced.

Goal for the month: A federated intrusion detector across simulated sites, and a measured effect of one poisoned client.

Before you start: setup for every project
  1. Set up a workspace. Create a private Git repository for the project with folders for data/, src/, figures/ and notes/. Make a Python virtual environment (python -m venv .venv) and record every package you install in requirements.txt.
  2. Keep a lab notebook. Add a dated entry to notes/log.md each time you work: what you ran (the exact command), what came out, and what surprised you. This log is the first thing to share when you ask for help, and most of your paper's method section comes from it.
  3. Make every number reproducible. Fix random seeds, write down the dataset version and how you split it, and keep the script that produced each figure next to the figure. If you cannot regenerate a number with one command, it is not finished.
  4. Stay legal and ethical. Work only with public datasets, data you recorded yourself with everyone's consent, or systems you own. Never test an attack on a network, car, radio band or service that is not yours, and follow the safety note on this page.
  5. When you get stuck. Spend about an hour, then write down what you tried, what you expected and the exact error or plot, and ask. A precise question is much easier to answer than "it does not work".
  1. Step 1 · Week 1Read the papers
    • Read the papers below, starting with federated averaging.
    • Write down how averaging works and how one bad client can pull the model.

    You are done when you can describe a poisoning attack in two sentences.

  2. Step 2 · Weeks 2 and 3Simulate ten sites
    • Follow the Flower quickstart, then switch it to simulation.
    • Split CICIoT2023 across ten simulated sites and train a shared detector.

    You are done when your federated model reaches a score close to one trained on all the data.

  3. Step 3 · Week 4Add a bad client
    • Make one client send scaled or flipped updates.
    • Compare mean and median aggregation with and without the bad client.

    You are done when one chart shows accuracy over rounds for the four cases.
