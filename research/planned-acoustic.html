Planned project · core area

Do earbuds, smart speakers and video calls leak what you type?

We showed in 2015 that one phone can tell which keys you press from their sound. Microphones are now in earbuds, speakers and every video call. This project measures how much they leak, and what stops it.

The question

Do earbuds, smart speakers and video calls leak what you type, the way a phone did in our 2015 attack?

Why it matters

People type passwords during calls every day, surrounded by more microphones than ever.

How we will test it

Record the same typing through each device and app, run a timing attack and a deep-learning attack, and switch each audio-processing stage on and off to see which one removes the leak.

Why this matters

Our MobiCom 2015 attack placed each keystroke by the tiny difference in when its sound reached a phone's two microphones, with no training data. Since then, deep learning has made keystroke recognition from recorded audio much easier, and the microphones around a keyboard have multiplied: wireless earbuds, smart speakers, laptops on video calls. Each of these processes sound differently, and some of that processing may blur or preserve the clues an attacker needs. Knowing which settings leak, and which simple defenses work, matters for anyone typing a password on a call.

Research questions

  1. How accurately can keystrokes be recovered from audio captured by earbuds, smart speakers and common video-call apps, compared with a phone next to the keyboard?

    Hypothesis
    Call compression and lower sample rates remove much of the timing detail ranging needs; attacks on spectral features degrade less.
    How we would test it
    Record the same typing sessions through each device and app, and run ranging and learning attacks on identical keyboards and typists.

  2. Which parts of each pipeline (compression, noise suppression, echo cancellation, stereo or mono) remove the clues that ranging and learning attacks need?

    Hypothesis
    Noise suppression and resampling remove the most; mixing down to mono removes ranging entirely.
    How we would test it
    Switch each processing stage on and off in an open audio pipeline and measure attack accuracy after each.

  3. Which cheap defenses, such as masking sounds or app settings, cut leakage without hurting the call?

    Hypothesis
    Quiet masking noise in the keystroke band cuts leakage with little cost to call quality.
    How we would test it
    Measure attack accuracy and a standard speech-quality score with each defense on.

Expected contributions

  • A side-by-side measurement of keystroke leakage through earbuds, smart speakers and call apps.
  • An account of which audio-processing stages remove the leak.
  • Cheap defenses with their cost to call quality.

Approach

  1. RecordCollect typing sounds on several keyboards with consenting volunteers and an IRB-approved protocol.
  2. PipelinesCapture the same typing through earbuds, a smart speaker and video-call software.
  3. AttacksRun a ranging attack like ours and a deep learning attack from the reading list.
  4. DefensesTest masking noise and app settings.
  5. ReportRecovery rates per device and setting, with clear advice for users.

Discussion

If it works

A measured answer to whether everyday audio leaks typing, and which settings stop it.

Where it could fail

  • Compression and noise suppression may remove the cues entirely, which would itself be good news.
  • Experiments must use consenting volunteers and made-up text.

How we will know

Keystroke recovery across devices, codecs and noise settings, next to the original phone attack.

Start here

A first month for a student joining this work, in 3 steps. Do them in order: each one uses what the one before produced.

Goal for the month: A comparison of how much keystroke detail survives a phone, earbuds and a video call.

Before you start: setup for every project
  1. Set up a workspace. Create a private Git repository for the project with folders for data/, src/, figures/ and notes/. Make a Python virtual environment (python -m venv .venv) and record every package you install in requirements.txt.
  2. Keep a lab notebook. Add a dated entry to notes/log.md each time you work: what you ran (the exact command), what came out, and what surprised you. This log is the first thing to share when you ask for help, and most of your paper's method section comes from it.
  3. Make every number reproducible. Fix random seeds, write down the dataset version and how you split it, and keep the script that produced each figure next to the figure. If you cannot regenerate a number with one command, it is not finished.
  4. Stay legal and ethical. Work only with public datasets, data you recorded yourself with everyone's consent, or systems you own. Never test an attack on a network, car, radio band or service that is not yours, and follow the safety note on this page.
  5. When you get stuck. Spend about an hour, then write down what you tried, what you expected and the exact error or plot, and ask. A precise question is much easier to answer than "it does not work".
  1. Step 1 · Week 1Read the papers
    • Read the papers below, including our keystroke ranging paper.
    • Note what each attack needs from the audio: timing, spectrum, or both.

    You are done when you can say which audio features each attack depends on.

  2. Step 2 · Week 2Record three ways
    • Type the same short text and record it through a phone, through earbuds, and through a video call you record on both ends.
    • Load all three with librosa and line them up in time.

    You are done when you have three aligned recordings of the same typing.

  3. Step 3 · Weeks 3 and 4Compare what survives
    • For each recording, measure how sharp each key press is in time and plot its spectrum.
    • Note which pipeline removes the most detail (noise suppression and compression are the usual suspects).

    You are done when one figure compares the three pipelines for the same key presses.

Papers to read first

  1. Keyboard acoustic emanations
    D. Asonov, R. Agrawal
    IEEE S&P, 2004
    The first paper to show keys can be told apart by sound.
    Read
  2. Keyboard acoustic emanations revisited
    L. Zhuang, F. Zhou, J. D. Tygar
    ACM CCS, 2005
    Recovers typed text without labeled training data, using language patterns.
    Read
  3. Context-free attacks using keyboard acoustic emanations
    T. Zhu, Q. Ma, S. Zhang, Y. Liu
    ACM CCS, 2014
    Uses several phones and timing differences, a close cousin of our work.
    Read
  4. Snooping keystrokes with mm-level audio ranging on a single phone
    J. Liu, Y. Wang, G. Kar, Y. Chen, J. Yang, M. Gruteser
    ACM MobiCom, 2015
    Our single-phone ranging attack, the starting point.
    Read
  5. A practical deep learning-based acoustic side channel attack on keyboards
    J. Harrison, E. Toreini, M. Mehrnezhad
    IEEE EuroS&P Workshops, 2023
    A recent attack that includes audio recorded over a video call.
    Read
