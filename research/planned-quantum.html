Does a quantum layer really help? Honest benchmarks for quantum intrusion detection

Hybrid quantum-classical models have reported strong intrusion-detection results in simulation. This project asks which part of that comes from the quantum layer, and what survives on real quantum hardware.

Why this matters

Our hybrid quantum-classical framework (EPJ Quantum Technology, 2025) detected DDoS traffic and classified Android malware with high accuracy in simulation, and a 2026 paper I co-authored ran a hybrid model on an IonQ quantum processor. A large 2024 benchmark study found that, on the small tasks it tested, out-of-the-box classical models outperformed the quantum classifiers overall, and that removing entanglement often did not hurt. Those findings set a high bar, and meeting it honestly is what makes a quantum security result credible.

Research questions

  1. Against classical twins with matched parameter counts and the same tuning budget, where does the quantum layer change detection accuracy, if anywhere?

    Hypothesis
    Once size, tuning budget and preprocessing are matched, most of the gap between quantum and classical layers falls within test-set noise.
    How we would test it
    Paired comparisons over many random seeds, with confidence intervals and McNemar's test, and the classical twin tuned as hard as the quantum model.

  2. How do the data encoding, the circuit depth and the number of qubits affect what the model can learn and whether it still trains?

    Hypothesis
    The encoding matters more than depth; deeper circuits run into flat training landscapes (barren plateaus) before they help.
    How we would test it
    Sweep encodings, depths and qubit counts, and track gradient variance during training next to accuracy.

  3. How much of the simulator result survives real hardware noise, and what does a run cost in time and money?

    Hypothesis
    Shallow circuits keep most of their simulated accuracy on current hardware, but cost and queue time rule out frequent retraining.
    How we would test it
    Run the same trained circuits on an ideal simulator, a noisy simulator and a quantum processor, and report accuracy, shots, wall time and cost.

Expected contributions

  • A fair comparison protocol for quantum and classical detectors, with matched budgets and statistics.
  • An open benchmark that runs on simulators and real quantum hardware.
  • Practical guidance on when a quantum layer is worth its cost.

Approach

  1. ReproduceRebuild our EPJ QT pipeline and its datasets in PennyLane or Qiskit.
  2. Twin modelsSwap the quantum layer for classical layers of equal size; remove entanglement.
  3. SweepVary encodings, depth and qubit count; watch for vanishing gradients.
  4. Add noiseUse hardware noise models, then run the best settings on a cloud QPU.
  5. ReportAccuracy, variance across seeds, wall-clock time and cost.

Discussion

If it works

A clear answer, whichever way it goes, on whether a quantum layer earns its place in intrusion detection.

Where it could fail

  • A null result is likely, and it is still worth publishing clearly.
  • Hardware noise and a small number of runs make comparisons statistically weak.

How we will know

Matched classical and hybrid models compared with confidence intervals, in simulation and on hardware.

Start here

A concrete first month for a student joining this work. Each step builds on the one before.

  1. Step 1Read the papers below, including the benchmark study that found classical models hard to beat.
  2. Step 2Rebuild one hybrid model in PennyLane and a classical twin with the same parameter count.
  3. Step 3Run both over repeated splits and report confidence intervals, not single numbers.
