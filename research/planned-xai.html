Explanations you can check: explainable intrusion and malware detection

Security analysts are asked to act on alerts from models they cannot see inside. This project tests whether today's explanation methods tell them the truth about why a detector fired, and builds explanations an analyst can check.

Why this matters

Explainability already runs through my applied AI work: our food fraud study (Expert Systems, 2025) compared LIME, SHAP and Google's What-If Tool on one model and showed what each can and cannot tell a food-safety analyst, and our employee attrition paper (LA-CCI, 2025) used explanations to find the factors behind people leaving. Security raises the stakes. An intrusion or malware alert has to be acted on quickly, different explanation methods can disagree about the same alert, and an explanation that looks convincing can still be wrong about what the model actually used. An honest explanation can also expose a detector that learned a shortcut, which links this project to the leak-free benchmark project.

Research questions

  1. For the same intrusion or malware detector, how often do LIME, SHAP and gradient-based explanations agree on the top reasons for an alert, and which of them are faithful to what the model really uses?

    Hypothesis
    Explainers agree on the top reason far more often than on the full ranking, and how faithful they are depends on the model family.
    How we would test it
    Measure rank agreement across explainers, and faithfulness with deletion and insertion tests, on several detectors and datasets.

  2. Can explanations reliably expose shortcuts, such as a detector keying on capture artifacts instead of attack behavior?

    Hypothesis
    Faithful explainers surface a planted shortcut reliably; unfaithful ones can hide it.
    How we would test it
    Plant known shortcuts in training data and count how often each explainer names them as a top reason.

  3. Where does a small interpretable model (rules or a shallow tree) match a black-box detector closely enough to replace it?

    Hypothesis
    On header-level features, a shallow tree or rule list matches the black box on most traffic types.
    How we would test it
    Distill each detector into trees and rule lists, and report fidelity to the original, accuracy and model size.

  4. Do explanations help analysts triage alerts faster and more accurately, or only make them more confident?

    Hypothesis
    Explanations speed up triage but can also make analysts more confident in wrong alerts.
    How we would test it
    A controlled study with students and practitioners triaging alerts with and without explanations, measuring time, accuracy and confidence.

Expected contributions

  • A faithfulness test suite for explanations of security models.
  • Evidence on whether explanations reveal shortcuts.
  • Small, readable detectors that match black boxes where they can, and a user study on analyst triage.

Approach

  1. DetectorsTrain intrusion and Android malware detectors on public datasets from our earlier work.
  2. ExplainGenerate LIME, SHAP and gradient-based explanations for the same alerts.
  3. MeasureScore faithfulness by removing top features, plus stability and sparsity.
  4. Shortcut huntPlant known artifacts in the data and see which methods find them.
  5. Analyst studyA small, IRB-approved study with student analysts triaging alerts.

Start here

A concrete first month for a student joining this work. Each step builds on the one before.

  1. Step 1Read the papers below.
  2. Step 2Train a malware or intrusion detector and explain its alerts with SHAP and a gradient method from Captum.
  3. Step 3Run a deletion test: remove the top features in ranked order and in random order, and compare.

Tools and data

  • SHAPExplanations based on Shapley values.
  • CaptumModel interpretability for PyTorch.
