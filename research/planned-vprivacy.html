Sharing car data without giving away where you have been

Connected cars share their position to make traffic maps and safety services work. A handful of those points can reveal where a driver lives and works. This project measures that risk and tests protections that keep the shared data useful.

The question

Can connected cars share position data for traffic services without revealing where their drivers live and work?

Why it matters

A few points in space and time are enough to single out most people, and every morning trace starts at the same house.

How we will test it

Re-identify pseudonymous traces in public mobility datasets, then apply noise, cloaking and trip-end hiding and plot re-identification against traffic-estimate error.

Why this matters

Location traces are among the most identifying data there is: a few points in space and time are enough to single out most people in a large mobility dataset. Connected cars report their position constantly, to traffic services, to automakers and to other cars. Removing names does not help much when every morning trace starts at the same house.

My dissertation at Rutgers was about making vehicle data available while keeping it private, in the group where my advisor, Marco Gruteser, had earlier introduced spatial and temporal cloaking for location privacy. Real-time traffic estimation at vehicular edge nodes (ACM/IEEE SEC 2017) showed what useful services need from the data. This project asks how little they can get by with.

Research questions

  1. How identifying are the traces that connected cars share, and how many points does an attacker need to link one to a home or workplace?

    Hypothesis
    A few points near the start and end of a trip are enough to link most traces to a home or workplace, even when names are removed.
    How we would test it
    Re-identify pseudonymous traces in public mobility datasets with simple home-and-work linking attacks, and report how the success rate grows with the number of points.

  2. Which protections keep traffic estimates useful while blocking re-identification: noise, cloaking, hiding trip ends or changing pseudonyms?

    Hypothesis
    Hiding trip ends and adding calibrated noise lowers re-identification sharply while road-level traffic estimates degrade only slowly.
    How we would test it
    Apply noise, cloaking, trip-end suppression and pseudonym changes at several strengths, and plot re-identification against the error of the traffic estimates built from the same data.

  3. Can the protection run on the car or at the roadside edge, so no central collector ever holds raw traces?

    Hypothesis
    Privacy filtering at the vehicle or roadside unit costs little compute and removes the need to trust a central collector with raw traces.
    How we would test it
    Implement the filters on embedded boards and measure delay and memory at realistic message rates.

Expected contributions

  • A re-identification test for shared vehicle traces.
  • A privacy-versus-utility map for common protections on traffic estimation tasks.
  • Edge implementations of the protections with run-time profiles.

Approach

  1. DataStart from public vehicle and mobility trace datasets.
  2. AttackLink pseudonymous traces to homes and workplaces with simple, realistic attacks.
  3. ProtectApply each protection at several strengths.
  4. Measure utilityRebuild traffic estimates from the protected data and measure their error.
  5. EdgeRun the best protections on embedded boards at realistic message rates.

The project uses public datasets and simulated traces only, never data that could identify a real person.

Discussion

If it works

Shared car data stays useful for traffic and safety while protecting where drivers go.

Where it could fail

  • Every privacy protection costs some usefulness, and the right balance depends on the use.
  • Re-identification attacks keep improving.

How we will know

Re-identification risk and usefulness for traffic tasks, for each protection setting.

Start here

A first month for a student joining this work, in 3 steps. Do them in order: each one uses what the one before produced.

Goal for the month: A measured home-inference attack on public GPS traces, and the privacy and accuracy cost of two defenses.

Before you start: setup for every project
  1. Set up a workspace. Create a private Git repository for the project with folders for data/, src/, figures/ and notes/. Make a Python virtual environment (python -m venv .venv) and record every package you install in requirements.txt.
  2. Keep a lab notebook. Add a dated entry to notes/log.md each time you work: what you ran (the exact command), what came out, and what surprised you. This log is the first thing to share when you ask for help, and most of your paper's method section comes from it.
  3. Make every number reproducible. Fix random seeds, write down the dataset version and how you split it, and keep the script that produced each figure next to the figure. If you cannot regenerate a number with one command, it is not finished.
  4. Stay legal and ethical. Work only with public datasets, data you recorded yourself with everyone's consent, or systems you own. Never test an attack on a network, car, radio band or service that is not yours, and follow the safety note on this page.
  5. When you get stuck. Spend about an hour, then write down what you tried, what you expected and the exact error or plot, and ask. A precise question is much easier to answer than "it does not work".
  1. Step 1 · Week 1Read the papers
    • Read the papers below, starting with my dissertation.
    • Note how each paper measures re-identification.

    You are done when you have one definition of success for the attack.

  2. Step 2 · Weeks 2 and 3Guess homes
    • Load GeoLife traces and split them into trips.
    • GeoLife has no home labels, so define a reference for each user: the place where they spend the most night-time hours.
    • Guess each user's home from trip start and end points alone, and check it against the reference.

    You are done when you report how often the guess lands within a set distance of the reference, and you chose that distance before looking at the results.

  3. Step 3 · Week 4Defend and measure
    • Add noise with OpenDP, or hide the first and last part of each trip.
    • Measure how often the home guess still succeeds, and how much a simple traffic count changes.

    You are done when one chart shows privacy against usefulness for each defense.
