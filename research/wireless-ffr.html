Sharing spectrum fairly: fractional frequency reuse

Before security, I worked on how cellular networks share spectrum. My M.S. thesis at Bilkent asked how to split a cell's subcarriers between users near the base station and users at the edge, so the network is both fast and fair.

The question

How should a cell split its spectrum between users near the tower and users at the edge?

Why it matters

Giving more to the center raises total speed but starves the edge; a network has to be both fast and fair.

What we found

Adapting the split to where users actually are gave about 5% higher throughput × fairness than the best fixed split.

The trade-off

Users near a base station have strong signals and can share the whole band with neighboring cells. Users at the cell edge hear the neighbors' interference too, so in fractional frequency reuse they are served on parts of the band that adjacent cells do not use at the same time. Every subcarrier given to the inner region raises total throughput but leaves less for edge users, so fairness falls.

The thesis measured both sides, cell throughput and Jain's fairness index, and used their product as one score for the trade-off. It compared static splits with adaptive ones that follow where users actually are.

The same physical-layer facts, shared spectrum and interference, sit under my later security work on GPS jamming and V2X messages.

Discussion

What it shows

Adaptive splits between the cell center and the cell edge improved the product of throughput and fairness by about 5% over fixed splits.

Where it falls short

  • The results come from simulation of WiMAX networks, a standard later replaced by LTE and 5G.
  • Real traffic and interference are less regular than the models.

What it means

The trade-off between speed for most users and fairness for the worst-served returns in 5G scheduling, and in fairness work on machine learning today.

Start here

A first month for a student joining this work, in 3 steps. Do them in order: each one uses what the one before produced.

Goal for the month: An ns-3 run that reproduces the fairness trade-off of fractional frequency reuse from the thesis.

Before you start: setup for every project
  1. Set up a workspace. Create a private Git repository for the project with folders for data/, src/, figures/ and notes/. Make a Python virtual environment (python -m venv .venv) and record every package you install in requirements.txt.
  2. Keep a lab notebook. Add a dated entry to notes/log.md each time you work: what you ran (the exact command), what came out, and what surprised you. This log is the first thing to share when you ask for help, and most of your paper's method section comes from it.
  3. Make every number reproducible. Fix random seeds, write down the dataset version and how you split it, and keep the script that produced each figure next to the figure. If you cannot regenerate a number with one command, it is not finished.
  4. Stay legal and ethical. Work only with public datasets, data you recorded yourself with everyone's consent, or systems you own. Never test an attack on a network, car, radio band or service that is not yours, and follow the safety note on this page.
  5. When you get stuck. Spend about an hour, then write down what you tried, what you expected and the exact error or plot, and ask. A precise question is much easier to answer than "it does not work".
  1. Step 1 · Week 1Redraw the cell plan
    • Read the thesis below.
    • Draw one cell with its inner and outer region and mark which part of the band each region uses.

    You are done when your drawing matches the thesis description and names the parameters you can change.

  2. Step 2 · Weeks 2 and 3Run the simulator
    • Install ns-3 and run one of its LTE examples with several cells (the LTE module includes a frequency-reuse example).
    • Find where the example sets the reuse scheme and the share of the band for cell centers.

    You are done when you can run the example from the command line and collect its throughput output.

  3. Step 3 · Week 4Sweep and plot
    • Vary the share of the band used in cell centers over several values.
    • For each value, compute total throughput and Jain's fairness index across users, and plot both on one chart.

    You are done when your chart shows the trade-off and is produced by one script.
