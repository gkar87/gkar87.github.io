The question

When a model makes a high-stakes call, such as flagging food fraud or predicting who will quit, can it show which inputs drove it?

Why it matters

Analysts and managers need reasons they can check, not a black-box score.

What we found

We compared LIME, SHAP and the What-If Tool on a food-fraud model, explained employee attrition, reviewed 68 crime-prediction studies and sorted Turkish scientific texts with 96% accuracy.

Explaining the model, not just scoring it

Food fraud detection

A deep learning model predicts food fraud risk from European rapid-alert notifications and an adulteration database, and three explanation tools (LIME, SHAP and Google's What-If Tool) are compared on it, showing what each can and cannot tell a food-safety analyst. Led by my M.Sc. student Okan Buyuktepe.

Discussion

What it shows

The methods carry across food fraud, employee attrition, crime prediction and Turkish scientific text, and comparing LIME, SHAP and the What-If Tool showed what each can tell an analyst.

Where it falls short

  • Explanation tools can disagree with each other and with what the model really relies on.
  • Each model was built on the data studied, and new data needs new validation.
  • Predictions about people, such as attrition or crime, raise fairness questions that accuracy does not answer.

What it means

An explanation should be checked before anyone acts on it. The checkable-explanations project turns that into a test.

Open research questions

Questions this work leaves open. They are directions, not results; each links to the planned project that takes it up.

  1. Are explanations faithful to what a model really uses, and do they help people decide better?

    Why it is open
    Our food-fraud work compared what LIME, SHAP and the What-If Tool can and cannot tell an analyst. Whether an explanation is faithful to the model is a separate test.
    Taken up in
    checkable explanations

  2. Can a retrieval-augmented chatbot be made robust to poisoned or misleading sources?

    Why it is open
    Our UCM chatbot answers from university pages; anyone who can get a page retrieved can try to steer it.
    Taken up in

  3. Can a language model help analysts triage security alerts without inventing facts?

    Why it is open
    Our chatbot work showed how a model grounded in retrieved pages answers questions. Security triage raises the cost of a confident wrong answer.
    Taken up in
    alert triage with language models

Start here

A first month for a student joining this work, in 3 steps. Do them in order: each one uses what the one before produced.

Goal for the month: An explained model on a public tabular dataset, and a short note on where two explanation methods disagree.

Before you start: setup for every project
  1. Set up a workspace. Create a private Git repository for the project with folders for data/, src/, figures/ and notes/. Make a Python virtual environment (python -m venv .venv) and record every package you install in requirements.txt.
  2. Keep a lab notebook. Add a dated entry to notes/log.md each time you work: what you ran (the exact command), what came out, and what surprised you. This log is the first thing to share when you ask for help, and most of your paper's method section comes from it.
  3. Make every number reproducible. Fix random seeds, write down the dataset version and how you split it, and keep the script that produced each figure next to the figure. If you cannot regenerate a number with one command, it is not finished.
  4. Stay legal and ethical. Work only with public datasets, data you recorded yourself with everyone's consent, or systems you own. Never test an attack on a network, car, radio band or service that is not yours, and follow the safety note on this page.
  5. When you get stuck. Spend about an hour, then write down what you tried, what you expected and the exact error or plot, and ask. A precise question is much easier to answer than "it does not work".
  1. Step 1 · Week 1Read the applied papers
    • Read the food-fraud and attrition papers linked on this page.
    • For each, note the question, the data, the model, and how the authors justified trusting it.

    You are done when you have a half page per paper.

  2. Step 2 · Weeks 2 and 3Train and explain
    • Pick a public tabular dataset and train a small scikit-learn model (a tree ensemble is a good start).
    • Explain ten individual predictions with SHAP and save the plots.

    You are done when each of the ten predictions has an explanation you can describe in one sentence.

  3. Step 3 · Week 4Compare explanations
    • Explain the same ten predictions with a second method (for example a gradient method from Captum on a small neural network, or a different SHAP explainer).
    • Mark where the two methods rank features differently, and suggest why.

    You are done when one table lists, for each prediction, the top features from both methods and whether they agree.

Tools and data

  • SHAPExplanations based on Shapley values.
  • CaptumModel interpretability for PyTorch.
  • scikit-learnClassical machine learning, pipelines and cross-validation.
