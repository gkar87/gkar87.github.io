Is the Internet of Things ready for post-quantum cryptography?

NIST published its first post-quantum cryptography standards in 2024. This project measures what moving to them costs on small IoT devices, and whether it quietly breaks the security tools that watch their traffic.

Why this matters

A large enough quantum computer running Shor's algorithm would break the RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography that protects almost all device traffic today. Because traffic can be recorded now and decrypted later, data with a long shelf life is already at risk. NIST's new standards, ML-KEM for key exchange (FIPS 203) and ML-DSA for signatures (FIPS 204), are built on lattices and use much larger keys and signatures. Servers and browsers can absorb that. Battery-powered sensors with a few hundred kilobytes of memory may not. My group works on both sides of this question: quantum machine learning for security, and identifying IoT devices and attacks from their packet headers, which change when handshakes grow.

Research questions

  1. What do ML-KEM and ML-DSA cost on common IoT boards in a real protocol (TLS or MQTT over TLS): handshake time, memory, energy and bytes on the air, alone and in hybrid with today's algorithms?

    Hypothesis
    Post-quantum key exchange is affordable on mid-range boards; signatures and certificate chains dominate the bytes on the air.
    How we would test it
    Measure TLS and MQTT-over-TLS handshakes on several boards with an energy meter, alone and in hybrid mode.

  2. Which devices and protocols cannot make the move without hardware changes, and what is the cheapest workable path for them?

    Hypothesis
    For the smallest radios, protocol changes such as cached or compressed certificates matter more than faster cryptography.
    How we would test it
    Map device classes against measured costs and test cheaper protocol options on the hardest cases.

  3. Do larger post-quantum handshakes confuse traffic-based tools, such as device fingerprinting and intrusion detectors trained on today's traffic?

    Hypothesis
    Fingerprinting and intrusion models trained on today's handshakes misread post-quantum ones until retrained.
    How we would test it
    Run trained models on traffic captured before and after the switch, and measure the drop and the retraining needed.

Expected contributions

  • Measured post-quantum handshake costs on common IoT boards.
  • A map of which devices can move now and which need protocol changes.
  • A test of how traffic-based security tools cope with post-quantum traffic.

Approach

  1. TestbedSet up ESP32 and Arm Cortex-M boards and a Raspberry Pi gateway running MQTT over TLS.
  2. IntegrateAdd ML-KEM and ML-DSA with open libraries such as liboqs and pqm4, alone and in hybrid mode.
  3. MeasureHandshake time, memory, energy and packet sizes per device.
  4. Check the watchersRerun our fingerprinting and intrusion detectors on post-quantum traffic.
  5. ReportA migration guide for IoT deployments, with open measurements.

Start here

A concrete first month for a student joining this work. Each step builds on the one before.

  1. Step 1Read FIPS 203 and the papers below.
  2. Step 2Build the Open Quantum Safe library and time key generation, encapsulation and decapsulation on a laptop.
  3. Step 3Repeat on a small board and count the radio frames a handshake needs.

Papers and standards to read first

  1. Polynomial-time algorithms for prime factorization and discrete logarithms on a quantum computer
    P. W. Shor
    SIAM Journal on Computing, 1997
    Why quantum computers threaten today's public-key cryptography.
    Read
  2. Post-quantum cryptography
    D. J. Bernstein, T. Lange
    Nature, 2017
    A short, readable overview of the replacement families.
    Read
  3. CRYSTALS-Kyber: a CCA-secure module-lattice-based KEM
    J. Bos, L. Ducas, E. Kiltz, T. Lepoint, V. Lyubashevsky, J. M. Schanck, P. Schwabe, G. Seiler, D. Stehlé
    IEEE EuroS&P, 2018
    The scheme that became ML-KEM.
    Read
  4. Module-lattice-based key-encapsulation mechanism standard (FIPS 203)
    National Institute of Standards and Technology
    NIST, 2024
    The key-exchange standard this project deploys.
    Read
  5. Module-lattice-based digital signature standard (FIPS 204)
    National Institute of Standards and Technology
    NIST, 2024
    The signature standard, which matters for firmware updates.
    Read
  6. pqm4: testing and benchmarking NIST PQC on ARM Cortex-M4
    M. J. Kannwischer, J. Rijneveld, P. Schwabe, K. Stoffelen
    IACR Cryptology ePrint Archive, 2019

    Read
  7. Benchmarking post-quantum cryptography in TLS
    C. Paquin, D. Stebila, G. Tamvada
