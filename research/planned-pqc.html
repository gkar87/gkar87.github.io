Is the Internet of Things ready for post-quantum cryptography?

NIST published its first post-quantum cryptography standards in 2024. This project measures what moving to them costs on small IoT devices, and whether it quietly breaks the security tools that watch their traffic.

Planned project · new direction · open to new graduate students

Why this matters

A large enough quantum computer running Shor's algorithm would break the RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography that protects almost all device traffic today. Because traffic can be recorded now and decrypted later, data with a long shelf life is already at risk. NIST's new standards, ML-KEM for key exchange (FIPS 203) and ML-DSA for signatures (FIPS 204), are built on lattices and use much larger keys and signatures. Servers and browsers can absorb that. Battery-powered sensors with a few hundred kilobytes of memory may not. My group works on both sides of this question: quantum machine learning for security, and identifying IoT devices and attacks from their packet headers, which change when handshakes grow.

Research questions

  1. What do ML-KEM and ML-DSA cost on common IoT boards in a real protocol (TLS or MQTT over TLS): handshake time, memory, energy and bytes on the air, alone and in hybrid with today's algorithms?
  2. Which devices and protocols cannot make the move without hardware changes, and what is the cheapest workable path for them?
  3. Do larger post-quantum handshakes confuse traffic-based tools, such as device fingerprinting and intrusion detectors trained on today's traffic?

Approach

  1. TestbedSet up ESP32 and Arm Cortex-M boards and a Raspberry Pi gateway running MQTT over TLS.
  2. IntegrateAdd ML-KEM and ML-DSA with open libraries such as liboqs and pqm4, alone and in hybrid mode.
  3. MeasureHandshake time, memory, energy and packet sizes per device.
  4. Check the watchersRerun our fingerprinting and intrusion detectors on post-quantum traffic.
  5. ReportA migration guide for IoT deployments, with open measurements.
