Can an attacker slip past the intrusion detector?

Machine-learning detectors can be fooled by small changes to their input. For network traffic, the attacker cannot change just anything: packets must stay valid and the attack must still work. This project measures how robust detectors really are against attackers who play by those rules.

Why this matters

Much of the work on adversarial attacks against intrusion detectors changes feature values freely, as if traffic were an image. Real traffic is not like that. A flood that is slowed down to evade a detector may no longer be a flood, and a packet field cannot take any value it likes. Results from unconstrained attacks can overstate the risk, and defenses tested only against them can give false comfort.

Our hybrid quantum-classical detector (EPJ Quantum Technology, 2025) showed a measured robustness advantage against adversarial perturbation. This project asks how much of that, and of the robustness claimed for other detectors, holds once attacks must respect what traffic can actually look like.

Research questions

  1. How much of the reported weakness of network intrusion detectors survives when attacks must keep the traffic valid and the attack working?

    Hypothesis
    Many feature-space attacks that look successful break the traffic they describe; once packets must stay valid and the attack must still work, far fewer evasions remain.
    How we would test it
    Attack the same detectors twice, once in feature space and once with constraints that keep the traffic valid and the attack functional, and compare evasion rates.

  2. Which defenses survive realistic attackers who adapt to them, and what do they cost in false alarms?

    Hypothesis
    Training on realistic adversarial traffic hardens a detector against the attacks it saw at a modest cost in false alarms; adaptive attackers will still find gaps.
    How we would test it
    Train detectors with and without adversarial examples, then attack them with adaptive attacks they did not see, reporting evasion and false-alarm rates together.

  3. Do compact and hybrid quantum-classical detectors resist evasion better than classical models of the same size?

    Hypothesis
    Our hybrid quantum work measured a robustness advantage against adversarial perturbation; part of it may come from model size and input encoding rather than the quantum layer.
    How we would test it
    Compare hybrid models against classical models with matched parameter counts and encodings under the same realistic attacks.

Expected contributions

  • A library of realistic, constraint-respecting evasion attacks on network traffic.
  • Re-measured robustness of common detectors when attacks must keep traffic valid.
  • Evidence on which defenses hold against adaptive attackers, and at what false-alarm cost.

Approach

  1. DetectorsTrain representative detectors on public intrusion datasets, including our hybrid models.
  2. ConstraintsWrite down what an attacker can change in each feature without breaking the traffic or the attack.
  3. AttackRun feature-space and constrained attacks side by side.
  4. DefendRetrain with adversarial examples and test against adaptive attacks.
  5. ReplayTurn the strongest attacks into real packets in a lab network and confirm they still work.

All attack traffic stays inside an isolated lab network.

Discussion

If it works

Intrusion detectors get an honest robustness number under realistic attacker rules.

Where it could fail

  • The rules that keep traffic valid are hard to encode completely.
  • Training for robustness can lower accuracy on normal traffic.

How we will know

Evasion success under constrained attacks, before and after defenses, and what each defense costs in accuracy.

Start here

A first month for a student joining this work, in 3 steps. Do them in order: each one uses what the one before produced.

Goal for the month: An attacked intrusion detector, and a comparison of what the attack achieves with and without realistic limits.

Before you start: setup for every project
  1. Set up a workspace. Create a private Git repository for the project with folders for data/, src/, figures/ and notes/. Make a Python virtual environment (python -m venv .venv) and record every package you install in requirements.txt.
  2. Keep a lab notebook. Add a dated entry to notes/log.md each time you work: what you ran (the exact command), what came out, and what surprised you. This log is the first thing to share when you ask for help, and most of your paper's method section comes from it.
  3. Make every number reproducible. Fix random seeds, write down the dataset version and how you split it, and keep the script that produced each figure next to the figure. If you cannot regenerate a number with one command, it is not finished.
  4. Stay legal and ethical. Work only with public datasets, data you recorded yourself with everyone's consent, or systems you own. Never test an attack on a network, car, radio band or service that is not yours, and follow the safety note on this page.
  5. When you get stuck. Spend about an hour, then write down what you tried, what you expected and the exact error or plot, and ask. A precise question is much easier to answer than "it does not work".
  1. Step 1 · Week 1Read the papers
    • Read the papers below, especially the one on attacks in the problem space.
    • Explain in your own words why changing features freely is not a real attack.

    You are done when you have a paragraph on feature space vs problem space.

  2. Step 2 · Weeks 2 and 3Train and attack
    • Train a detector on CIC-IDS2017.
    • Attack it with the Adversarial Robustness Toolbox (for example fast gradient or projected gradient methods) and record how many attacks are missed.

    You are done when you have detection rate before and after the unconstrained attack.

  3. Step 3 · Week 4Attack under real limits
    • List which features an attacker can really change (packet sizes and timing) and which follow from them.
    • Repeat the attack changing only those, and compare.

    You are done when one table compares no attack, unconstrained attack and realistic attack.

Tools and data
